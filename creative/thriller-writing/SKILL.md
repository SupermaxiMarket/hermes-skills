# Thriller Writing — for Pierre Andre Erard

Use when writing, editing, or reviewing chapters of a thriller novel. Covers structure, tension arcs, character voice, dialogue, and pacing.

## Core Principles

1. **Every sentence pushes plot or tension** — no fluff, no filler. If a paragraph doesn't raise stakes or reveal character, cut it.
2. **Show, don't tell** — no "he felt afraid". Show the sweat on the glass, the tremor in the hand, the decision delayed by one second too many.
3. **Rythme cardiaque** — alternate high-tension scenes with slower breathers. A reader can't sustain peak adrenaline for 300 pages. Give them valleys so the peaks hit harder.
4. **Characters drive plot** — every twist must grow from a character's choice, not coincidence. The antagonist isn't evil; they're right from where they stand.
5. **Research is invisible** — the detail that sells the scene (how a silencer really works, what forensics can actually detect) must be correct but never explained. Trust the reader to follow.

## Structure — The Classic Thriller Arc

### Act I — The Incipit (15-20%)
- **Opening hook**: First page must establish voice, place, and jeopardy. Start mid-action, never with weather or backstory.
- **The ordinary world**: Brief — just enough to know what's at stake when it breaks.
- **Inciting incident**: A death, a discovery, a message, a face from the past. Something that makes retreat impossible.
- **First threshold**: The protagonist commits to the chase/investigation/escape. No turning back.

### Act II — The Labyrinth (50-60%)
- **Rising action**: Clues found, witnesses killed, allies appear and die. Each chapter ends on a micro-cliffhanger.
- **Midpoint reversal**: A revelation that inverts everything — the killer isn't who we thought, the victim isn't dead, the conspiracy goes higher than imagined.
- **The dark night**: All seems lost. The protagonist isolated, betrayed, out of options.

### Act III — The Resolution (20-25%)
- **Final confrontation**: Not a monologue. The antagonist reveals their logic through action. The hero wins by being smarter, not stronger.
- **The twist in the tail**: Last 3 pages — one final turn that reframes the entire story. Leave the reader staring at the wall.
- **Epilogue (optional)**: Max 2 pages. The cost of winning. What the hero lost.

## Tension Toolkit

| Technique | Effect | When to use |
|-----------|--------|-------------|
| **Dread** | Reader knows something the protagonist doesn't | Mid-act II, to build towards climax |
| **Countdown** | External deadline (bomb, trial, rendezvous) | Throughout, as spine |
| **Red herring** | False suspect, misdirected investigation | Act II, to keep reader guessing |
| **Cliffhanger** | Chapter ends on unanswered question | Every chapter end |
| **Ticking clock** | Scene-level time pressure | Action sequences |
| **Limited POV** | Reader only knows what protagonist knows | Full novel — maximizes surprise |
| **Multiple POVs** | Reader sees pieces protagonist doesn't | Switching between hunter/hunted |

## Character Archetypes for Thriller

- **The Burned Professional** — ex-cop, ex-spy, ex-soldier. Haunted by one case. Drinks. Has rules he's about to break. (Harry Hole, Jack Reacher, Lisbeth Salander)
- **The Ideologue** — killer/criminal with a code. Believes he's the hero. His logic is consistent and terrifying. (Hannibal Lecter, John Doe in SE7EN)
- **The Innocent** — witness/victim who knows too much. Needs protection. May not survive. (Classic damsel, but give her agency)
- **The Mentor with Secrets** — guide who helps but is not what he seems. May be the real villain. (The usual suspect)
- **The Bureaucrat** — official who blocks the investigation. Not evil, just following procedure. Represents the system's failure.

## Dialogue Rules

1. **No greetings** — cut "Hello", "How are you", small talk. Every line advances plot or character.
2. **Subtext** — what they don't say matters more. "Nice weather" in a thriller means "I don't trust this room."
3. **Interruptions** — characters cut each other off. Real conversations overlap.
4. **Silence is a line** — "He didn't answer" is a stronger reply than any excuse.
5. **Voice differentiation** — the cop speaks clipped, factual. The academic speaks in paragraphs. The killer speaks in riddles or silence.

## Pacing by Chapter Length

| Scene Type | Target Word Count | Vibe |
|------------|------------------|------|
| Action / chase | 800-1,200 | Breathless, short paragraphs, one-sentence paragraphs |
| Investigation / dialogue | 1,500-2,500 | Medium pace, back-and-forth |
| Revelation / confrontation | 2,000-3,000 | Building, then explosion |
| Aftermath / breather | 1,000-1,500 | Slow, reflective, deceptive calm |
| Chapter end | Always < 100 words after the turn | The hook for next chapter |

## French Thriller Specifics

- Use **passé simple** in narrative, **imparfait** for description, **présent** for action sequences to heighten immediacy.
- French police/justice terminology must be accurate: juge d'instruction, garde à vue, crime organisé (not "organised crime"), PJ vs DGSE vs DGSI.
- Setting matters: Paris is a character (the Seine at dawn, the 18th at night, the tunnels under the city). Use real geography.
- Avoid "anglicismes de traduction" — a thriller set in France must feel French, not like a dubbed American movie.

## Inspiration Stacks

- **Jean-Christophe Grangé** — for labyrinthine plots, shocking openings, historical depth
- **Dennis Lehane** — for moral ambiguity, Boston grit, characters who bleed
- **James Ellroy** — for staccato prose, paranoia, the underworld as ecosystem
- **Michael Connelly** — for procedural realism, Bosch's code, the patient build

## Pitfalls

- Don't explain the twist — let the reader connect the dots. If you have to explain it, it didn't work.
- Don't let the protagonist be passive — they must chase, not just be chased.
- Don't kill a character just for shock — death must serve plot or theme.
- Don't use amnesia, "it was all a dream", or "the butler did it". These are banned.
- Don't over-describe — one perfect detail per scene is enough. "The room smelled of wet ashes and old tea."

## Verification

- Read the last chapter first. If it's not compelling alone, the build didn't earn it.
- Cut the first 50 words of every chapter. The real start is usually later.
- Read dialogue aloud. If it sounds stiff written, it's unbearable spoken.
